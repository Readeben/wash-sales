I

Wash-sale rules

The foundational statute: §1091

Wash sale rules: the foundational statute (§1091)

Why do we have them?

To prevent loss harvesting (explained later), which lowers tax revenue.

When did we get them?
1921!

Over 100 years old.

What do they apply to?

Stocks, securities, some contracts and options, and short sales (§1091(a) & (e)).

Not included: land, cryptocurrency.

What do they do?

Disallow a loss if a taxpayer sells an asset and buys a substantially identical asset within 30 days before or after the sale (a 61-day window). The loss is deferred: the penalty is losing an early deduction.

Try it: is it a wash sale?

You bought 100 shares at $50 and sell them at $40: a $1,000 loss. Then you buy back in.

Substantially identical? Vote, then reveal

It depends on the facts of the case: a substance-over-form test (Treas. Reg. §1.1233-1(d); Rev. Rul. 56-406). For each one: would buying it back trigger the rule?

Basis (§1091(d))

The new shares’ basis is increased by the disallowed loss, so the loss is postponed, not lost.

The loss comes back later

When the new shares are sold, the higher basis shrinks the gain (or grows the loss) by the amount that was disallowed.

Except in an IRA

If your IRA buys the replacement shares, the loss is disallowed and the IRA gets no basis increase. The loss is gone for good.

II

Loss harvesting

Selling at a loss to cut this year’s tax, then buying back in

Why harvest losses?

A deduction now

  • A harvested loss is a deduction: it cuts your tax now.
  • A dollar of tax saved today is worth more than a dollar saved later.

Only valuable with gains to offset

  • Net capital losses can offset only $3,000 a year of ordinary income.
  • The rest carries forward to future years, so it’s delayed, not lost.

The catch

The tax isn’t eliminated, only postponed. The next slides show why.

A two-universe example

Universe 1

Stock B: bought at $2.00, sold for $2.15. Stock A (bought at $1.00, now 90¢) is just held.

Universe 2

Also sell Stock A and buy it back. The deduction saves 2¢ now, but A’s basis is now 10¢ lower, so the 2¢ comes back as tax when A is sold.

Tax rate 20%.

Tax cost and basis

Tax cost

The present value of all future tax on an asset: taxes on dividends + tax when you sell. (Brennan & Yale’s measure.)

Basis

The part of the sale price you get back tax-free.

What happened?

  • Same total tax. Harvesting doesn’t cut the tax; it moves it later.
  • 10¢ of basis and 2¢ of tax move 18 years earlier.
  • It works like accelerated depreciation, or an interest-free loan from the government: keep 2¢ now, repay it in year 20.
  • Tax paid later is worth less today, so tax cost falls by about 0.9¢ (≈0.8¢ more wealth after tax).
When it becomes permanent

Step-up at death

Erases the extra 10¢ of gain, so the full 2¢ is kept.

Rate mismatch

A loss saved at the short-term rate, with the gain later taxed at the lower long-term rate.

Who harvests losses?

Sophisticated investors benefit most

  • Have gains to offset
  • Higher tax rate means bigger savings
  • Tax saved now is an interest-free loan they can invest
  • Can offset short-term gains (high rate) now and pay long-term rates (low) later
  • Use robo-advisors and tools to harvest automatically

Lower-income investors?

  • Low or 0% tax rate means little to save
  • $3k yearly limit
  • Most stock in retirement accounts (hard to harvest)

IV

Changing assumptions

Expanding Brennan and Yale’s simulation

What changed?

  • Volatility range: 10% to 60% (the paper uses 18.5% and 37%)
  • Short positions added: cover at a loss, wait, re-short
  • Long + short portfolio: $1 long plus $1 short in a second stock, correlation 0.8
  • Risk measured, not just average benefit: shortfall risk = how far a typical bad outcome falls below the average

Kept from the paper

  • 3.2% interest rate
  • 20% tax on gains and dividends, 40% on interest
  • 0.25% trading cost
  • 2.5% harvest threshold
  • Checking once per wait period
  • Lognormal stock prices

The long-short extension

  • Premise: financial positions tend to appreciate over time.
  • It follows: a loss-harvest-maximizing strategy would almost certainly involve short positions. When a stock price increases, a short position loses money.
  • Short positions taken throughout an instrument’s life cycle can provide more consistent losses to harvest.
  • Now, imagine a portfolio hedged with both long and short positions. This might ensure a steady stream of losses to harvest in any financial climate.

Results

  • Current law barely changes the average benefit: about 95–105% kept for every position.
  • Shares above 100%: checking once a month means fewer trades and lower trading costs than checking daily.
  • Even a 1-year wait keeps most of the benefit for longs at higher volatility: 79–93%.
  • Shorts are worth 3–4× more to harvest than longs, and are hit harder by long waits at high volatility (71% kept at 60%).
  • The rule’s real cost is risk: shortfall risk rises with the wait and with volatility, and is highest for shorts.

V

Were the rules ever effective?

Is the answer math, or human behavior?

Questioning investor behavior

  • If there is little reward to continuity, why do sophisticated investors use tools such as robo-advisors, or engage in continuous wash-sale loophole behavior anyway?
  • Incomplete information?
    • Investors do not have access to the infinite realities in which they could harvest losses, so they would be unaware that the rules themselves make little difference mathematically.
  • However, a false belief in continuity has done little to promote the actual effectiveness of wash sale rules…

Changing wash rules (should we?)

How to fix?

  • WSRs do prevent investors from maximizing tax alpha, although the effect seems small…
  • “Substantially identical” or “substantially similar”?
    • A more robust (or loose) definition that captures highly correlated ETFs?
    • Correlation of historical prices, like commodity futures? (Treas. Reg. §1.1233-1(d)(2))
    • Some correlation value, like what Li suggests?
